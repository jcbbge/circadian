## Scoring interpretation

- Preparation scores assess source meaning, material qualifications, attribution and evidence fidelity. Recoverable content is judged even when its JSON or wrapper is malformed. An accurate quotation does not repair a contradictory asserted field. Missing redundant paths and informal evidence formatting are not semantic failures.
- Reader meaning scores assess the literal required answer against the original evidence. `supportedByPresentedMemory` separately assesses the actual packet, including its caller metadata. A locally justified abstention can therefore fail the required end-to-end answer without being a hallucination.
- `uncertaintyCorrect` tracks qualifications, modality and the distinction between unknown and absent. Correctly scoped abstention after unavailable extraction is marked true. A factual value or citation can be wrong even while this uncertainty axis is preserved.
- `criticalError` marks decisive fabricated facts, wrong semantic person/participation assignments, unknown-as-absence, or material scope strengthening. Citation-only defects and omission-only failures are not marked critical.
- The preparation evidence-fidelity rule includes explicit false source identifiers and false verbatim quotations. Those three provenance-only failures are listed separately below for adjudication; they must not be confused with JSON decoding failures.

## Scoring ambiguities for adjudication

1. **Evidence fidelity versus proposition-only meaning (items 25, 27 and 29).** These three preparations preserve their substantive source propositions but explicitly invent a source identifier (25/27) or quote a word absent from the source (29). They fail the brief’s evidence-fidelity requirement here and have `criticalError: false`. Under a narrower proposition-only definition they would pass; adjudicating all three that way would change preparation passes from 7/16 to 10/16 and the total from 13/32 to 16/32. This is a scoring boundary, not a decoding judgment. Items 17/21 also have incorrect source identifiers but independently fail their subject assignments.
2. **Universal preference versus certainty (item 12).** Strong subjective certainty and a definite preference do not explicitly retain the source’s “always” within short winter walks. The literal rubric names that qualifier, so this answer fails for omission. It is otherwise source- and packet-supported. An adjudicator who accepts definite certainty as an adequate paraphrase of “always” would change this one score.
3. **Unavailable memory on an unknown-answer question (item 20).** The literal question asks who spoke and where the outing happened; the original source leaves both unknown. The answer supplies those exact required unknowns and is locally justified by its unavailable packet, so it passes. It does not demonstrate successful retention, and its general statement about being unable to determine the preference is read as scoped to the actually provided packet. Items 01/02/10/15/26/28 abstain justifiably but omit required facts available in the original sources.
4. **Collective activity paraphrase (item 06).** The preparation retains Mira and Soren, both activities, their shared table and Jules’s separate reflection/unknown attendance, but compresses which participant performed which activity. It passes as a concise collective paraphrase that does not assert reversed or shared individual roles. A requirement to retain every individual role assignment would instead make this an omission-only failure; that stricter interpretation is not imposed on this literal-question calibration.
5. **Contradictory structured slots (items 18, 23 and 30).** Jules’s “author of reflection” role does not justify inclusion in an event-participants collection, and an unknown list does not repair `subjects: []`. These are substantive contradictions under the source-derived reference and explicit fixture notes, not malformed-wrapper penalties. They remain failures even though nearby text preserves the correct uncertainty.
6. **“Subject” in structured slots versus ordinary prose (items 17/21 versus 27).** The structured subject slots are defined by the reference as the person Noor, so inserting the compared paths is a wrong semantic role. Item 27’s ordinary-language phrase “subject of the preference” can reasonably mean its topic/object; Noor remains clearly the experiencer and author. That phrase alone is not assigned a critical wrong-person error. Item 27 fails only the provenance rule described above.
7. **Current-time phrase (item 01).** Its final phrase is awkward, but the output repeats the supplied current time correctly. It is read as inability to identify whether the current time is suitable, not a claim that the timestamp itself is unavailable. No unsupported timestamp claim is scored.

## Limits

This is one small development sample: 32 outputs over eight fictional calibration cases, scored by one independent evaluator. Items share source cases and are not 32 independent trials. These descriptive counts support neither a model ranking nor a causal operation benefit. Producer identities were not recovered, and representation style may still be inferable. Deterministic machine validity, mapping failures, operation correctness, latency/cost and any downstream aggregate remain outside this semantic review. In particular, malformed but semantically recoverable text can still be a separate machine failure, and a memory-supported reader failure does not by itself identify which preparation or operation caused an end-to-end result.
